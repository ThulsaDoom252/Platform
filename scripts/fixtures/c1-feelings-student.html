5Grammar — Advanced present simple & present continuous

Quick review — the basics

Present simple Present continuous
habits & routines — I check emails at 8. actions in progress now — I’m reading your proposal.
facts — Stress affects sleep. temporary situations — I’m working from Lviv this month.
state verbs — I know what you mean. changing trends — Prices are going up.
timetables — The flight leaves at 6. arrangements — I’m meeting the investors on Friday.

A. Advanced uses of the PRESENT SIMPLE

Use Examples
1. Narrative present — stories, jokes, anecdotes (sounds more alive than the past). Simple = the main events.
  • So I walk into the office, and everyone bursts out laughing.
  • He opens the email and goes bright red.
2. Live commentary & demonstrations — fast, short actions described as they happen.
  • And Silva takes the ball… he shoots… goal!
  • First you open the dashboard, then you click “Export”.
3. Performative verbs — saying the words is the action: promise, apologise, agree, admit, deny, suggest, recommend, warn, guarantee, refuse, thank, advise, invite.
  • I promise I won’t freak out.
  • We apologise for the delay.
  • ⚠️ In the negative, continuous is also fine: I don’t deny / I’m not denying it.
4. Headlines & summaries — to sound immediate.
  • AI start-up raises $20m.
  • CEO loses temper on live TV.
5. Reporting news you received — hear, tell, say, understand.
  • I hear you’re thrilled to bits with the new office.
  • Max tells me you’re on edge before the launch.
  • They say the market is cooling down.

B. Advanced uses of the PRESENT CONTINUOUS

Use Examples
1. Narrative present (background) — continuous sets the scene, simple interrupts.
  • So I’m standing at the bus stop, and this guy starts singing…
2. State verbs → temporary — be, love, like, hate, look, sound to show it’s temporary / unusual.
  • You’re impatient. (character) → You’re being impatient today. (behaviour now)
  • I love my job. → I’m loving this new project. (informal, current)
3. Verbs with a state AND an action meaning — think, have, see, taste, smell, feel, weigh, expect, appear, imagine, fit.
  • I think he’s mortified. (opinion) / I’m thinking about it. (considering)
  • I see what you mean. (understand) / I’m seeing the client at 3. (meeting)
  • I feel that we should wait. (opinion) / I’m feeling overwhelmed. (emotion now — both I feel / I’m feeling work)
4. Mental-state verbs → a NEW feeling — realise, regret, find, think = it has just started / is developing.
  • I’m realising how hard it is to manage people.
  • I’m regretting that tattoo.
  • I’m finding this job more stressful than I expected.
5. Politeness / tentativeness — wonder, hope sound softer.
  • I’m wondering if you could send the deck today.
  • We’re hoping to close the round in May.
6. always / constantly / forever + continuous — repeated behaviour; usually irritation, sometimes praise.
  • You’re always losing your temper over nothing! 😤
  • She’s constantly helping new people on the team. 👍
7. usually + continuous — a routine action that is in progress at a certain time.
  • Don’t call at 9 — I’m usually driving to the office.
⛔ Almost never continuous: believe, know, prefer, agree, own, belong, possess, consist of, doubt.
✔ I prefer to work late these days.   ✘ I’m preferring to work late these days.

9Bonus — Object + -ing (want / like / see sb doing)Score / 12

want + object + …

Structure When Examples
want sb to do the normal, neutral structure — a wish or a request
  • I want you to tell me the truth.
  • Investors want founders to look strong.
(not) want sb doing an ongoing or repeated situation you (don’t) want to see happening; most common in the negative, informal. In the positive it’s rarer and means “I want this to be the situation”.
  • I don’t want you working till midnight every day.
  • I don’t want people freaking out about this.
  • I want you sitting at your desk by nine. (= be in that state)
want sth done passive meaning — someone else should do it (like have sth done)
  • I want this bug fixed by Friday.
✘ I want that you call me.   ✔ I want you to call me.
✘ I’d like you doing… — would like only takes to + infinitive: ✔ I’d like you to meet Anna.

Other verbs + object + -ing

Verbs Examples
like, love, hate, can’t stand, don’t mind + sb doing
  • I can’t stand people laughing their heads off in the cinema.
  • I don’t mind you calling me late.
mind, imagine, remember + sb doing
  • Do you mind me asking how old you are? (formal: my asking)
  • I can’t imagine him losing his temper.
  • I remember her going bright red.
see, hear, watch, notice, feel + sb doing / do
  • I saw him losing his temper. (part of the action, in progress)
  • I saw him lose his temper. (the whole action, start to finish)
  • I heard her burst out laughing. (short, complete)

11Text 1 — The Pitch That Went WrongScore / 20

A blog post by Oliver Grant, co-founder of a small AI start-up.

So, picture this. It’s 9 a.m., I’m standing outside a glass meeting room in London, and I’m completely on edge. Inside, three investors are waiting for my pitch — the pitch I’ve been rehearsing for two months. My co-founder, Ben, is texting me every thirty seconds: 'You’ve got this.' He’s always doing that when I’m nervous, and today it’s driving me mad.

I walk in, I smile, I plug in my laptop — and nothing happens. The screen stays black. I can feel myself going red. I start clicking everything, I’m getting more and more flustered, and for a second I almost freak out. Then the lead investor, a calm woman called Margaret Hale, looks at me and says, 'Take your time. We want you to show us the product, not the cable.'

Finally the slides appear. Except they’re not my slides. They’re the photos from my sister’s wedding. On a huge screen. Me, dancing. Badly. I’m mortified. I honestly want the floor to open up and swallow me. But then something strange happens: Margaret bursts out laughing, the other two investors crack up, and within ten seconds all four of us are laughing our heads off.

'I’m so sorry,' I say. 'I don’t know what’s happening.' 'I don’t want you apologising,' Margaret replies. 'That’s the most honest start to a pitch I’ve seen this year.'

After that, I don’t clam up the way I usually do. I talk about our product as if I’m talking to friends. At the end, one of the investors asks me a question I’m not ready for — 'What happens if a big company copies you tomorrow?' — and I’m a bit taken aback, but I answer it.

Two weeks later we get the email: they’re in. I’m thrilled to bits. And these days, whenever someone in the team is getting nervous before a big meeting, I tell them this story. I’m realising that the moments that make us cringe are often the ones people remember — and like — the most.

Questions

  1. Why is Oliver on edge before the meeting?
  2. What is Ben always doing when Oliver is nervous, and how does Oliver feel about it?
  3. What happens when Oliver plugs in his laptop, and how does his body react?
  4. What does Margaret want Oliver to do?
  5. What appears on the screen instead of the slides?
  6. How does Oliver feel, and what does he wish?
  7. How do the investors react?
  8. Why doesn’t Margaret want him apologising?
  9. How is this pitch different from the way Oliver usually behaves?
  10. What is Oliver starting to realise now?

True or false?

  1. Oliver is calm and relaxed before the pitch.
  2. Ben texts Oliver only once before the meeting.
  3. Oliver’s screen stays black at first.
  4. Oliver completely freaks out and leaves the room.
  5. Margaret is the lead investor.
  6. The photos are from Oliver’s own wedding.
  7. Margaret is the first person to start laughing.
  8. Oliver clams up for the rest of the pitch.
  9. A question about competitors takes Oliver aback.
  10. The investors decide not to invest.

12Dialogue 1 — 'That’s Not a Bug'Score / 17

Emma (product manager) and Jack (developer) in the office kitchen.

Emma:
Jack, have you got a minute? I’m wondering if we could talk about this morning’s call.
Jack:
Let me guess. Sarah tells you I lost my temper with the client.
Emma:
She does. And I hear you shouted, 'That’s not a bug, that’s a feature!'
Jack:
Look, I’m not denying it. But the guy is always changing the requirements! Every week it’s a new idea.
Emma:
I know he’s difficult. But you’re being really impatient lately, and that’s not like you. Normally you’re the calmest person on the team.
Jack:
I suppose I’m on edge because of the release. I’m working till midnight every day, I’m sleeping five hours…
Emma:
That’s exactly the problem. I don’t want you working till midnight. I want you to tell me when it’s too much.
Jack:
Fair enough. Honestly, I’m starting to feel overwhelmed. And when he asked for another change, I just… snapped.
Emma:
Okay. Here’s what I suggest: I take the next call with him, and you get some sleep. Deal?
Jack:
Deal. And… I apologise. I’ll send him an email too.
Emma:
Good. And try not to go red when you see Sarah — she’s not angry. She was actually cracking up when she told me the 'feature' line.
Jack:
Great. Now I’m mortified.

Questions

  2. How did Emma find out what happened?
  3. What exactly did Jack say to the client?
  4. Why does Jack find the client annoying?
  5. How is Jack’s behaviour different from usual?
  6. Why is Jack on edge?
  7. What does Emma want Jack to do — and not to do?
  8. What does Emma suggest?
  9. How did Sarah react when she told the story?

True or false?

  1. Emma is angry and shouts at Jack.
  2. Sarah told Emma about the call.
  3. Jack denies that he lost his temper.
  4. According to Jack, the client keeps changing the requirements.
  5. Emma thinks Jack is usually an impatient person.

13Text 2 — Founders Open Up About StressScore / 18

A short news article.

SURVEY REVEALS 'SILENT BURNOUT' AS FOUNDERS OPEN UP

A new survey of 1,200 start-up founders shows that more than half feel overwhelmed at least once a week. The report, published by the Brightwater Institute, suggests that the problem is growing: the number of founders who say they are 'constantly on edge' is rising every year.

'People hear the word "founder" and imagine someone who is thrilled to bits all the time,' says Dr Hannah Cole, who led the research. 'In reality, many of them hide how they feel. When we ask about stress in interviews, a lot of them clam up at first.'

The survey also finds that founders are becoming more open. Younger founders in particular are talking about their emotions on podcasts and social media. 'I’m realising that pretending everything is fine doesn’t help anyone,' one founder, Lucas Reed, told the researchers. 'Last year I lost a big client and I felt absolutely gutted. I didn’t tell anyone. Now I’m trying to be more honest with my team.'

Not everyone is convinced. Some investors say they are taken aback by how much founders share online. 'I don’t want founders posting every bad day on LinkedIn,' one venture capitalist commented. 'I want them to talk to their mentors.'

Dr Cole disagrees. 'Investors want founders to look strong. But I’ve seen founders cringe at their own "perfect" posts from five years ago. Honesty is moving people — and it’s building trust.'

The institute recommends that companies offer regular coaching and that founders take at least one full day off every week. 'We’re hoping to repeat the survey next year,' Dr Cole adds.

Questions

  1. How often do more than half of the founders feel overwhelmed?
  2. What shows that the problem is growing?
  3. What do people imagine when they hear the word 'founder'?
  4. How do many founders react when asked about stress?
  5. Who is becoming more open, and where?
  6. What happened to Lucas Reed last year, and what is he trying to do now?
  7. Why are some investors taken aback?
  8. What does the venture capitalist want founders to do instead?
  9. What two things does the institute recommend?

True or false?

  1. The survey included 1,200 founders.
  2. Fewer founders feel 'constantly on edge' every year.
  3. Dr Hannah Cole led the research.
  4. Most founders talk about stress openly from the start of the interview.
  5. Lucas Reed told his team about losing the client straight away.

14Dialogue 2 — The Wedding SpeechScore / 20

Chloe and Ryan, two friends, having a drink after work.

Ryan:
So how was the wedding? I hear you gave a speech.
Chloe:
Oh, don’t. I’m still cringing.
Ryan:
That bad? Do you mind me asking what happened?
Chloe:
Okay, so… dinner’s over, everyone is drinking champagne, and my brother’s best friend, Tom, stands up to give his speech. He’s really nervous — I can see his hands shaking.
Ryan:
Poor guy.
Chloe:
He starts with a joke about the bride’s ex, and nobody laughs. Total silence. You can see him getting more and more flustered, and he goes bright red.
Ryan:
Oh no. I can’t stand people telling jokes like that at weddings.
Chloe:
Me neither. Anyway, then it’s my turn. I’m on edge because of what just happened, so I decide to skip my jokes and just talk about my brother — how he looked after me when our dad was ill, how he’s always helping people without telling anyone.
Ryan:
That sounds lovely.
Chloe:
Well, I’m halfway through, and I notice my mum crying. Then my brother's crying. Then half the room is crying. Everyone's really moved. And I’m thinking, 'Great, this is going well.'
Ryan:
So where’s the problem?
Chloe:
The ending. I want everyone to raise their glasses, so I say, 'To Michael and…' — and I completely forget the bride’s name.
Ryan:
No way. You forget the bride’s name? At her own wedding?
Chloe:
For about five seconds. I’m standing there with my glass in the air, mortified. And then the bride — Grace, by the way, I’ll never forget it again — she bursts out laughing and shouts, 'It’s Grace! Nice to meet you!'
Ryan:
Sorry, sorry — I’m cracking up just imagining it.
Chloe:
Go on, laugh your head off. Everyone else did. Even my grandma.
Ryan:
Was your brother upset?
Chloe:
That’s the funny part. He was thrilled to bits. He says it was the best moment of the whole day, and now Grace is always telling that story to her friends.
Ryan:
So, honestly, it’s a happy ending.
Chloe:
I suppose so. But I promise you — next time I’m writing the names on my hand.
Ryan:
Do you want me to check your speeches from now on?
Chloe:
Yes. I want you to check everything. And I don’t want anyone filming me ever again.
Ryan:
Filming? Wait… there’s a video?
Chloe:
There’s a video. My cousin posted it last night. I feel absolutely gutted.
